ABSTRACT

010

011

Video editing has become essential in digital media creation, yet existing automated

012

systems are restricted to short segment processing and domain-specific tasks. They

013

face two critical limitations: i) inability to handle diverse video comprehension and

014

editing operations, and ii) lack of long-video understanding for coherent narrative

015

creation. We propose VideoAgent, an all-in-one agentic framework addressing

016

these challenges through two key innovations. First, we develop automated video shot creation with shot planning agents for coherent narratives and cross-modal

017

retrieval for aligned visual content. Second, we design a multi-agent orchestration

018

framework integrating over thirty specialized editing agents. Intent parsing filters

019

relevant tools while self-reflective graph orchestration assembles complex editing

020

pipelines. Extensive experiments on our newly-proposed VideoEdit benchmark

021

and public datasets demonstrate VideoAgent's superiority over existing multimodal

022

LLMs and agentic systems. VideoAgent achieves 87-98% orchestration success

023

rates while reducing API costs by 60%. Human evaluation across six video

024

categories shows VideoAgent produces professional-quality content approaching

025

human-level performance, with ratings only 4% below human-created videos.

027 1 INTRODUCTION
028

029 Video editing has become indispensable in modern digital media creation (Esser et al., 2023), as 030 evidenced by the explosive growth of platforms like YouTube and TikTok where millions of creators 031 produce content daily (Chai et al., 2023). However, traditional video editing requires complex

032 professional tools, specialized skills, and intricate multi-step workflows that create significant barriers
033 for ordinary users. This gap between widespread content creation demand and technical accessibility 034 has driven the need for automated video editing systems (Wang et al., 2025) that can interpret natural 035 language instructions and execute sophisticated editing tasks without requiring professional expertise.

036 Recent advances in AI technology have made it increasingly feasible to leverage Large Language 037 Models (LLMs) (Minaee et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025) and Vision Language

038 Models (VLMs) (Zhang et al., 2024b; Li et al., 2022) for multimodal data understanding and editing.

039 Deep Video Discovery (DVD) employs LLM-coordinated tool ensembles to enable autonomous

040 video exploration through systematic "observe-reason-act" cycles (Zhang et al., 2025). VideoRAG

041 introduces a sophisticated dual-channel architecture combining knowledge graphs with multimodal

042

encoding for complex reasoning over long videos (Ren et al., 2025). mPLUG-2 demonstrates modular multimodal foundations using dual visual encoders for unified text, image, and video understand-

043 ing (Xu et al., 2023b). Though these works have made pioneering explorations on multimodal

044 content understanding and editing, none of them have adequately solved the complex requirements of

045 automated video editing. These existing approaches suffer from two critical limitations: first, they

046 typically focus on specific understanding or editing tasks and cannot handle the diverse range of

047 video comprehension and editing operations required in real-world scenarios. Second, they often

048 lack the long-video understanding and planning capabilities essential for human-level video editing,

049 preventing them from creating videos with sufficient length and coherent narrative structure.

050 To address these limitations and achieve human-level automated video creation capabilities, we 051 identify two fundamental challenges that must be overcome:
052
053 � Coherent Long-Form Video Planning. Professional video creation demands coherent narratives that span multiple shots and extended durations, requiring sophisticated top-down shot planning

2.1 VIDEO EDITING TASK FORMALIZATION

119

120 We formally define the video editing task as follows. Given i) a natural language instruction I

121 describing the desired video editing objectives, and ii) a collection of multimodal content resources

122 M = {m1, m2, . . . , m|M|} (e.g., raw video clips, audio tracks, and textual documents for narration), 123 the system produces a complete edited video V that satisfies the specified instruction I. The output

124 video V comprises a temporally ordered sequence of video shots V = (v1, v2, . . . , v|V |), where each 125 shot vi represents a coherent video segment containing synchronized visual and audio components. 126 In essence, the video editing task is to build an agentic system f as follows:

127

f : (I, M)  V = (v1, v2, . . . , v|V |)

(1)

128

129

2.2 AUTOMATED VIDEO SHOT CREATION

130

131

2.2.1 SHOT PLANNING AGENT

132 To generate the video shot sequence (v1, v2, . . . , v|V |), we develop a shot planning agent that produces 133 shot-level storyboards in natural language. Each storyboard provides a textual description specifying
134 the visual content for the corresponding shot, ensuring all shots collectively form a coherent narrative
135 fulfilling the video creation instruction. This process requires global awareness: the shot planning 136 agent must understand both the overall story structure specified by the user instruction I and the 137 available visual resources M. Given these requirements, the shot planning agent operates as follows:

138

S = (s1, s2, . . . , s|V |) = ShotPlanning(I, M, |V |) = LLM(I, C, |V |; ps)

139

C = LLM(c1, c2, . . . , c|M|; pc), ci = Caption(KeyFrames(mi))

(2)

140

where S is the generated shot sequence, C is the compressed visual context summarizing all available

141 142

materials, ci is the caption for the i-th visual material, ps and pc are prompts for shot planning and visual context generation respectively, and |V | is the desired number of shots.

143

144 The shot planning agent operates through two sequential LLM processing steps. First, it processes

145 all keyframe captions to generate a compressed visual summary C that captures the available video 146 materials. Second, it combines this visual summary with the user instruction I to generate the required 147 number of shot descriptions that form the overall narrative structure.

148

2.2.2 SHOT VISUAL CONTENT RETRIEVAL

149

150 Given the shot-level storyboards S, VideoAgent employs paired video indexing and retrieval modules 151 to identify and fetch appropriate video clips for each shot in an efficient manner. The indexing module

152 first extracts keyframes from each video segment using the KeyFrames method mentioned above, then

153 employs cross-modal representation methods such as ImageBind and CLIP to generate embeddings

154 for these keyframes within a unified text-visual representation space. For retrieval, VideoAgent gen-

155 erates embeddings for each shot's storyboard si using the same cross-modal representation approach,

156

then selects the visual content with the highest cosine similarity to the storyboard embedding for use in the current shot. Formally, this indexing and retrieval process works as follows:

157

158

ri = arg maxj cos(ej, Embed(si)), ei = Embed(KeyFrames(mi)), i = 1, 2, . . . , |V | (3)

159 where ri represents the retrieved material index for the i-th shot, ei denotes the embedding for the 160 i-th material's keyframes, and cos(�, �) denotes cosine similarity. With this method, our visual content
161 retrieval module fetches semantically aligned visual content to form a coherent narrative following the foregoing text-based shot planning.

2.2.3 FINE-GRAINED VIDEO TRIMMING

174
175 Though the previous modules fetch semantically aligned visual materials, these raw materials are trimmed with uniform length. In real creative work, shot lengths are variable to match the rhythm
176 of background music and dialogue duration (see Listing 9 for shot length decision agents). To 177 address this issue, VideoAgent further designs a fine-grained video trimming agent that employs 178 Vision Language Models (VLMs) with visual understanding capabilities to select the most suitable
179 sub-segment from the retrieved video clip vri based on the current shot's storyboard text, serving as 180 the final visual material for that shot, formally as follows:

181

182

vi = mri [tstarti : tendi ], [tstarti , tendi ] = VLM(mri , si, ti+1 - ti; pt)

(4)

183

184 where vi is the final trimmed video segment for the i-th shot, mri is the retrieved raw material, tstarti

185 186

and tendi are the start and end timestamps for trimming, ti+1 - ti represents the target duration for the shot, and pt is the prompt for video trimming guidance. Through this adaptive trimming design,
the system achieves precise temporal alignment between visual content and narrative requirements.

2.3 VIDEO EDITING WITH AGENT GRAPH ORCHESTRATION

190

191

2.3.1 MULTIMODAL EDITING AGENT LIBRARY

192 To enhance VideoAgent with advanced video editing capabilities beyond simple concatenation, 193 we develop a multi-agent system for sophisticated video editing operations. This system enables 194 complex creative transformations requiring deep understanding of content semantics, artistic styles,
195 and technical production workflows. The system encompasses two primary categories of tool agents:

196 � Foundational multimodal processing tools that provide essential technical operations such as

197

music beat extraction, voice synthesis, face swapping, lip-sync generation, and text translation.

198

199

� Creation prompts with human expertise that offer guidance for content generation including style-specific scriptwriting, lyrical composition, and meme-based humor creation. For example, the

200

system can transform English stand-up routines into Chinese crosstalk, generate derivative meme

201

content, or synchronize video cuts with background music rhythms.

202
203 These diverse tools are essential for enabling flexible and creative video production workflows, and to support this broad spectrum of editing requirements, we develop a comprehensive Agent Library
204 covering a large array of creation genres. Notably, the functionalities used in aforementioned video 205 shot creation (Section 2.2) are also wrapped as tool agents, ensuring all capabilities are unified within

206 this multi-agent system framework. Details about all our tool agents are given in Appendix A.5.

207

208

209

2.3.2 EDITING AGENT GRAPH

210 To capture the complexity of editing workflows required by advanced video editing, our VideoAgent 211 proposes to compose the tool agents into an agent graph. In this graph, nodes represent individual
212 agents while edges capture information flow, representing the sequential dependencies of agents
213 processing data. Formally, the tool agent library is denoted as A = {A0, A1, . . . , An}, and the 214 graph-structured agent workflow is represented as G = {A, E, }, where E  A � A denotes 215 directed edges encoding inter-agent dependencies (e.g., Ai  Aj), and  : E   defines the state
transition function for parameter passing between agents, where  denotes the parameter space.

2.3.3 EFFICIENT AGENT SELECTION VIA INTENT PARSING

217

218 To achieve efficient agent selection for each creation instruction I, VideoAgent employs an intent
219 parsing mechanism to filter and prioritize relevant agents. Given instruction I, the system reads the 220 agent registry R containing parameter specifications and functional descriptions, and uses LLMs 221 to decompose both explicit and latent sub-intents within I based on a predefined intent-to-agent 222 mapping table M . This process can be defined as:

223

S0 = LLM(I, R, M ; piparse), R = (Ai | A)

(5)

224 Here S0 denotes intents extracted from the user instruction, which correspond to a set of agents 225 usually used to solve these intents. piparse is the prompt for intent parsing, and  is the registry 226 function that generates JSON schema formatted registration tables for LLM comprehension.

2.3.4 SELF-REFLECTIVE AGENT GRAPH ORCHESTRATION AND EXECUTION

229

230 To more accurately compose suitable agent graphs for solving user instruction I, VideoAgent adopts

231 a self-reflective workflow for agent graph Orchestration. The process begins with the intent parsing

232

stage, where it performs iterative self-reflection and revises its intent parsing outputs. This selfreflection process considers whether the extracted intents can form an agent graph sufficient to solve

233 the user instruction. Formally, it works iteratively as follows:

234

235

Si+1, Fi+1 = LLM(I, Si, Fi; piref )

(6)

236 where Si represents the intent set at iteration i, Fi denotes the feedback from the previous iteration, 237 and piref is the prompt for iterative reflection. Through this iterative self-reflection, more accurate 238 intent parsing results are obtained for efficient agent pre-selection. The process has a maximum 239 iteration limit, with the LLM controlling whether continued reflection is necessary.

240 Subsequently, for the workflow planning stage, we design a two-step self-evaluation mechanism: 241 the first step assesses whether the current graph requires further modification, while the second step
242 reflects and generates a new iteration of G. This approach enables precise identification of graph

243 deficiencies and addresses potential failure modes, which is defined as follows:

244 245

ri =LLM(Fi, I, R, Gi; pgref ), Gi+1, Fi+1 = LLM(I, S, Fi, Gi; pgref )

(7)

246 where ri represents the assessment result at iteration i, Fi denotes the feedback for graph reflection, R

247 is the agent registry, and pgref , pgref are the prompts for graph assessment and revision respectively.

248 Agent Graph Execution. After constructing the agent graph G, the execution follows the graph249 defined workflow through sequential agent invocation. The execution process begins by identifying

250 entry nodes (agents with no incoming edges) and proceeds through topological ordering of the graph

251 structure. Formally, the execution process can be described as:

252

253

Oj = Aj(Ij, j), Ij =

((Ai, Aj))(Oi)

(8)

(Ai,Aj )E

254 where Ai, Aj represent agents in the graph, I, O refer to input and output data, respectively. j 255 represents the agent-specific configuration parameters. The state transition function ((Ai, Aj)) 256 transforms the output from agent Ai into the appropriate input format for agent Aj according to the

257 edge specification. The execution continues until all agents in the graph have been processed and the

258 final video editing outputs are generated from the terminal nodes (agents with no outgoing edges).

444 4 RELATED WORK

445 Automated Multimodal Content Editing integrates textual, visual, and temporal modalities to
446 enable intelligent content manipulation. Recent advances in diffusion-based models have facilitated
447 text-guided image editing through cross-modal attention mechanisms (Shuai et al., 2024; Fang et al., 448 2025), while video editing research has addressed temporal consistency via spatiotemporal frame449 works incorporating multiple conditional inputs (He et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025). Multimodal large 450 language models have further enabled systems that parse complex editing instructions and execute
corresponding visual operations (Zhang et al., 2024a; Cheng et al., 2025). However, contemporary re451 search primarily emphasizes editing granularity and temporal coherence while overlooking workflow 452 orchestration challenges. Our work addresses comprehensive workflow orchestration by integrating 453 over thirty specialized agents that adaptively assemble editing workflows based on user instructions, 454 enabling seamless execution of complex multi-step tasks without manual coordination.
455
456 Multi-Agent Systems. Recent advances in multi-agent systems have shifted from monolithic AI models to collaborative frameworks where multiple autonomous agents interact to solve complex
457 tasks (Zhuge et al., 2024a; Tang et al., 2025a). Key developments include the use of LLMs to enable 458 natural language reasoning and tool invocation. This paradigm demonstrates remarkable flexibility 459 through dynamic role specialization, where agents adaptively assume functions such as evaluation and 460 refinement (Zhuge et al., 2024b), program synthesis and execution (Tang et al., 2025b), and seamless 461 interfacing with external APIs, databases, and computational resources (Shi et al., 2024). However,
462 existing multi-agent systems primarily target text-based applications like coding and mathematics.
463 Our work extends multi-agent frameworks to multimodal video editing workflows.

464 Video Understanding and Retrieval are intrinsically connected pillars of visual intelligence, with 465 advances in one domain driving progress in the other. Modern research in video understanding 466 focuses on developing specialized models for challenges like visual question answering (VQA) (Qian 467 et al., 2024; Sima et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025), leveraging complex fusion mechanisms to extract
468 spatial and temporal semantics. Representative works such as VideoMind (Liu et al., 2025) and
469 Qwen-2.5-VL (Bai et al., 2025) demonstrate strong capabilities in long-video scenarios, enabling
470 precise temporal localization and spatio-temporal reasoning. Complementarily, retrieval-oriented 471 methodologies leverage cross-modal video-text models that learn aligned representations in shared 472 embedding spaces. Techniques like VideoRAG (Ren et al., 2025) construct comprehensive knowledge 473 graphs integrating diverse long-video sources. Our work differs by designing a framework with
strong video understanding and retrieval capabilities specifically for video editing workflows.

477 We presented VideoAgent, a comprehensive agentic framework that unifies video understanding

478 and editing capabilities to enable automated video creation across diverse genres and production

workflows. Our approach addresses two fundamental challenges in automated video editing: coherent long-form video planning through global-aware shot creation, and multi-agent workflow orchestration

481 demonstrates significant superiority over existing methods, achieving 87-98% orchestration success

482 rates while reducing MLLM API costs by 60%. Human evaluation confirms that VideoAgent produces

484 VideoAgent as an effective solution for bridging the gap between widespread content creation demand

485 and technical accessibility, paving the way for more sophisticated automated creative systems.

486 ETHIC STATEMENT
487

488 VideoAgent democratizes video creation by lowering technical barriers, enabling broader creative 489 expression and content production across diverse user communities. However, we acknowledge that 490 automated video editing capabilities could potentially be misused to create misleading or manipulative
491 content that spreads misinformation. To mitigate these risks, we recommend implementing content

492 moderation frameworks and encourage responsible usage practices among users and platforms. LLMs 493 were used sparingly for language refinement in the preparation of this manuscript.

506 USAGE OF LLMS
507

508 In this work, large language models (LLMs) serve as key components across multiple editing agents to 509 enhance understanding, planning, and execution for adaptive video generation aligned with user goals.

510 Specifically, 1) LLMs compress visual keyframe captions and integrate them with user instructions to
511 generate coherent shot-level storyboards, ensuring global narrative consistency; 2) Vision-language 512 models adaptively select precise video segments guided by storyboard text and target durations, 513 achieving fine-grained temporal alignment with narrative rhythm. Additionally, 3) LLMs parse user 514 instructions to extract intents that facilitate efficient agent selection and prioritization from a registry,
while 4) dual-stage self-reflective agent graph orchestration iteratively refines intent understanding 515 and evaluates workflows to improve agent coordination and mitigate failures. Furthermore, LLMs are 516 employed in a limited capacity for language refinement during manuscript preparation.

A.1 VIDEO SHOWCASE

727

728 Below are video showcases we created with VideoAgent, which can be grouped into six main 729 categories: Beat-synced Edits, Video Overviews, Storytelling, Meme Video Edits, Song Remixes, 730 and Cross-lingual Adaptations (including stand-up comedy and crosstalk adaptations).

731 Beat-synced Edits: The user provides background music and film footage. VideoAgent automatically 732 detects beat and tempo changes in the music and aligns high-energy film visual shots with strong beat 733 moments. Video Overview: The user provides video footage of a news item or event. VideoAgent 734 transcribes the speech, writes a summary of the event, matches the meaning of each summary
735 sentence to corresponding visual segments from the original video, and generates voiceover for each
736 sentence to complete the edit. Storytelling: The user provides plain text of a novel or story along

737 with video footage for visual matching. VideoAgent scripts the story in a specific narration style,

738 matches each line of the script to suitable video segments based on semantic meaning, and generates

739 voiceover for each script segment to produce the final video. Meme Video Edits: The user supplies

740 a source video and a custom script or narrative. VideoAgent extracts and transcribes the original

741

audio, generates new speech, precisely syncs the replacement audio to video frames, and outputs a professionally dubbed, meme-style edit. Song Remixes: The user provides a MIDI file, lyrics,

742 background music, and a target voice sample. VideoAgent generates a cover, clones the target voice,

743 aligns timing with the arrangement, and integrates the result into the video pipeline for a polished,

744 synced remix. Cross-lingual Adaptations: The user provides source audio and target voice samples.

745 VideoAgent adapts the script into the desired cultural format (e.g., Chinese crosstalk or English talk

746 show), synthesizes target voices, applies appropriate effects, and integrates the result into the video

747 editing pipeline for a polished, synced adaptation.

A.2 EVALUATION DATASETS AND PROTOCOLS

1082 To evaluate VideoAgent in terms of both video understanding and workflow orchestration, we conduct

1083 experiments from the following two perspectives.

1084

1085 � Video Understanding and Retrieval Evaluation. We utilize the Shot2Story dataset to evaluate

1086

whether VideoAgent can effectively retrieve appropriate video clips according to user inputs. This

dataset contains video clips paired with corresponding queries that describe each clip's content. We

1087

randomly select 100 videos with more than 5 queries each, using the midpoint frames as keyframes

1088

for retrieval evaluation. In total, there are 599 individual queries. Note that achieving such global

1089

understanding and precise retrieval across multiple video segments is computationally intensive.

1090

Processing all queries for a multimodal LLM or agent typically requires around 3-9 hours. The

1091

distribution of evaluation video durations in our sampled dataset is shown in Figure 12. Videos

1092

are grouped into fixed intervals 10�15s, 15�20s, 20�25s, 25�30s, and 30s+ based on their total

1093

segment lengths. The caption queries per video distribution in our dataset is illustrated in Figure 13.

1094

Each video in the dataset contains between 5 and 8 captions, providing multiple query perspectives

1095

for evaluation. For video categories distribution in our sampled dataset is shown in Figure 14.

1096

The dataset exhibits a diverse range of content categories, with Entertainment, Film & Animation,

1097

Autos & Vehicles, Sports , News & Politics, Howto & Style, Science & Technology, Comedy,

Education, Travel & Events, People & Blogs, Gaming and Pets & Animals of the sampled videos.

1098

1099

We use three metrics to compare retrieved video clips and groundtruth shots: i) Recall: This is the

1100

percentage of groundtruth videos being retrieved with the first rank for each query. ii) Embedding

1101

Matching Score: Following Shi et al. (2021); Yang et al. (2024); Cheng et al. (2025), we compute the coarse-grained embedding matching score between retrieved video content and LLM-generated

1102

captions (see List 6) using ImageBind Girdhar et al. (2023) cross-modal encoder. This evaluates

1103

whether retrieved videos align with user intents at a semantic level. iii) Intersection over Union:

1104

Following Team et al. (2025), we compute temporal IoU between predicted and ground-truth video

1105

segments. IoU measures the overlap between retrieved and target clips, ranging from 0 to 1.

1106 � Workflow Orchestration Evaluation. To assess the model's performance in orchestrating work-

1107

flows for video editing instructions, we construct the VideoEdit dataset containing 2000 creation

1108

instructions encompassing diverse visual and audio editing requirements. Starting with authentic

1109

A.4 IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS

1439 This section will provide detailed descriptions of the implementation details and parameter configura1440 tions for each baseline in different experiments.

1441 All baseline methods are configured using their default or recommended parameter settings and 1442 implemented following the original papers. All experiments were conducted on NVIDIA RTX 3090 1443 GPUs. Workflow Orchestration Evaluation: In the workflow orchestration performance comparison
1444 and workflow orchestration ablation study, for fairness, we use Claude-Sonnet-3.7 as the Judge Agent

1445 to evaluate Agent Graphs and Agent Chains from multiple perspectives, including execution sequence

1446 validation, parameter routing correctness, agent functional redundancy assessment, and requirement

1447 fulfillment analysis. We also set the number of reflection rounds to 2 for all self-reflective methods.

1448 In the LLM-human judgment consistency experiment, to reduce the error rate of human judgment, we

1449

first execute agent graph checks for execution sequence validation and parameter routing correctness issues, then proceed with agent functional redundancy assessment and requirement fulfillment

1450 analysis. Video Editing Evaluation: All video inputs are segmented into three-second segments,

1451 under fps1, one frame is sampled per second. For recall calculations, the midpoint timestamp of each

1452 retrieved video segments serves as the reference point. Subsequently, two-second clips are extracted,

1453 one second before and one second after the midpoint timestamp, yielding a total duration of two

1454 seconds per retrieved clip. These extracted clips are then concatenated sequentially to generate the

1455 final output video for computing the Embedding Matching score and Intersection over Union metrics,

1456 all ours VLM agent/caption use MiniCPM-V 2.6 int4 Yao et al. (2024). In the video editing ablation
1457 study and hyperparameter experiments, all our base models utilize GPT-4o, and continue to keep the video segments divided into three seconds for input.
